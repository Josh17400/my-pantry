## What to build

### 1. ~300 canonical ingredients

Cover a real US home kitchen: produce, dairy, meat/seafood, pantry staples, grains/pasta,
canned goods, baking, spices/herbs, condiments/sauces, oils/vinegars, frozen, beverages,
baby/household-adjacent edibles. Weight coverage toward what people **actually buy weekly** —
this seeds real usage, not a culinary encyclopedia.

Per ingredient: `id`, `name`, `category` (drives grocery aisle grouping), `allergens[]`,
`isStaple`, `defaultFormId`, plus common `aliases` (including receipt-style abbreviations you
are confident about — `PARM`, `SHRD CHDR`, `GRND BF`).

### 2. Forms, conversions, packages

Where an ingredient realistically exists in multiple forms, model them: garlic
(whole/clove/minced/powder), cheese (block/shredded/grated), butter (stick/tbsp/g), onion
(whole/chopped), herbs (fresh bunch/chopped/dried). Give each form `dim` and, where it applies,
`densityGPerMl` or `gramsPerCount` with an honest `uncertaintyPct`.

`PackageSpec` entries for typical US retail sizes — these seed par levels, so they matter:
14.5 oz can diced tomatoes (with `drainedG`), gallon of milk, 5 lb flour, dozen eggs, 8 oz
block of cheese.

### 3. Data quality rules — the part that actually matters

- **Cite your density sources** in a comment or a `source` field. USDA FoodData Central is the
  preferred reference. **Do not invent numbers that look plausible.** A wrong density is
  undetectable by tests and corrupts inventory silently.
- Where a value is genuinely variable (a "bunch" of cilantro varies 2–4× by store and season),
  give it a **high `uncertaintyPct`** rather than false precision. Honest uncertainty is the
  correct answer; a confident wrong number is not.
- **Allergens must be right.** Under-tagging is a safety failure: soy sauce contains wheat,
  Worcestershire contains fish (anchovy), many baking chips contain milk and soy, "natural
  flavors" is not a free pass. Tag conservatively — over-tagging is merely annoying,
  under-tagging can hurt someone.
- **EDGE DIRECTION INVARIANT (enforced, not advisory):** emit each conversion edge in **one
  direction only.** `convert()` auto-inverts non-`oneWay` edges, so emitting both directions
  creates parallel arcs whose factors can drift apart under rounding and make conversions
  path-dependent. Mark genuinely lossy conversions `oneWay: true` (whole chicken → boneless
  yield cannot be inverted). **Write a validator that fails the test suite if seed data
  contains both directions of the same form pair.**

### 4. Validation module + tests

Ship `validateSeed()` alongside the data, and test that the shipped seed passes it:

- every `defaultFormId` resolves to a real form of that ingredient
- every `ConversionEdge` references forms that exist
- **no duplicate-direction edges** (the invariant above)
- no duplicate ingredient ids or colliding aliases across different ingredients
  (a collision makes matching ambiguous — fail loudly)
- densities within a physically sane band (roughly 0.1–2.0 g/ml; nothing is denser than
  honey or lighter than puffed rice by much) — catches typos and unit slips
- every ingredient has at least one form, and forms have the field their `dim` requires
- spot-check tests asserting specific known conversions land in the right ballpark
  (1 cup granulated sugar ≈ 200 g, 1 cup all-purpose flour ≈ 120 g, 1 large egg ≈ 50 g,
  1 stick butter = 113 g)

## Conventions

- Pure TypeScript data modules, **zero React / react-native / platform APIs**, `strict`, no
  `any`. No new runtime dependencies.
- Keep the data readable and diffable — split across category files rather than one enormous
  array, with a barrel at `src/seed/index.ts`.
- Deterministic ids (stable slugs like `garlic`, `garlic-clove`), never generated at runtime.
